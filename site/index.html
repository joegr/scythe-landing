scythe

Cut cards the way they should be cut.

Scythe is a workspace for debate evidence. Your team cuts, marks up and files cards, writes cases from them, and prints both, on a laptop, phone or tablet. Each team has its own private Scythe. Every card is the author’s exact words, and no teammate’s work is ever lost.

Principles

  1. A card is the author’s words. Scythe saves a card only if its text appears in the source, word for word. Paraphrase is impossible, and no AI ever edits a card.
  2. What is marked is what is read. Marks cover whole words and never drift. The read-aloud version is exactly the marked text.
  3. No one’s work is overwritten. When two people edit a card at once, Scythe shows both versions and lets you keep either or combine them. Every earlier version is kept.
  4. Every card has a resolution. Every past NSDA topic in Policy, LD, PF and Big Questions is built in, and teams can add their own.
  5. Everything prints. Each card and each case is a single plain text file, and every printout is made from that file. Paper always matches the screen.

How it works

  • Open a source. Paste a link and Scythe reads the page and drafts the cite: author, date and publication. If a site turns Scythe away, it says so and what to do: paste the text, or use the “Read in Scythe” bookmark, which sends the page open in your own browser straight into Scythe. Paywalled article or PDF? Paste the text; Scythe repairs the line breaks. A page on paper? Take a photo.
  • Cut. Select the passage, or double-click to take a whole paragraph; selections snap to whole words as you make them. Write the tag and check the cite. A card is at most 400 words, and one source gives at most ten cards.
  • Mark. Select words and press 1, 2 or 3. Marks belong to the source, so every card cut from the same words shows them.
  • File. Place the card in a file, such as Aff or Neg, and a block, such as Inherency or a DA link. Approve it once it is cut, checked and ready for rounds.
  • Link. Record which cards answer or support one another. Each card lists its answers, which form its frontlines.
  • Search. Type as you would into a search engine. Exact phrases rank first, suggestions appear as you type, and results sort by relevance, recency or date published.
  • Search by person. Select a name in a card to see every card that quotes that person or was cut from their work.

Built for paper

Some tournaments and leagues limit laptops or internet access in round. Scythe is designed so that a team can debate from paper alone.

  • Every unit prints. One card; a card with all its answers, as frontlines; a whole file, as a speech document; or a case.
  • Black and white is enough. Marks print as underlines of different weights: thin for underline, heavy for highlight, double for emphasis. No color printer is needed.
  • Print the full card or only what you read. The read-aloud version prints the marked words alone.
  • The printout is the record. Every page is generated from the same file the team edits, so the paper copy is never out of date.
  • Paper comes back in. Scan a stack of printed cards with a phone or tablet: each is read back in a few seconds as a card, marks included, and waits for you to check it while you scan the next.
  • Word, when it’s allowed. Any card copies into Word with Verbatim formatting.

Cases

A plain editor for writing a case, with the team’s cards alongside it. Select words in a card and they enter the case as that card’s exact text, linked to their source. Scythe rejects any quotation that does not match. Lines take Verbatim’s heading levels: pocket, hat, block and tag. If a partner saves first, Scythe names them and lets you keep either version. A case prints the same way a card does, from the single file it is saved in.

Security and trust

  • A private Scythe for each team. Cards, their history and the team’s members are stored on the team’s own Scythe and nowhere else. No other team can see them, and no list of teams exists: a team’s Scythe is found only with one of its codes.
  • Membership by invitation. No one can create an account without an invite code from a team admin. A personal invite expires after two weeks; a school code, for a whole squad of up to 500, lasts a year. Either can be revoked sooner. A new member sees none of the team’s work until an admin lets them in, and every sign-in expires after 30 days.
  • Roles. Admins, usually coaches, invite and remove members and appoint other admins. Removing a member signs them out on every device at once. A team always keeps at least one admin.
  • Sign-in. Members sign in with a passkey, using Face ID, Touch ID or a device passcode, so there is no password to steal. Email and password is also available; passwords are stored only as an irreversible hash, and repeated wrong attempts on an account are limited, to stop passwords being guessed by repeated tries.
  • Scanned pictures. A team’s Scythe sends each scanned picture over an encrypted connection to Scythe’s reading service, which every team shares. It reads the picture in memory, stores nothing, keeps no record of what it read, and sends back only the words and their marks. In the iPhone app, the picture is read on the phone itself.
  • Accountability. Every edit is recorded under the username of the member who made it, and each card’s full history is visible.
  • Student privacy. Histories show usernames only, never names or email addresses.
  • A polite reader. When Scythe opens a link, it tells the site plainly that it is one person’s reader, not a crawler, follows the site’s rules, and never works around a block. Nothing it reads is kept for AI training.

Devices

  • Any browser. The same cards and history on a laptop, phone or tablet. On an iPhone, Scythe can be added to the Home Screen and opens like an app. A native iPhone app is in testing.
  • Scanning. On a phone or tablet, “Scan a card” opens the camera inside Scythe with a frame to fit the card in, as an ID scanner does. Scythe outlines the card as it finds it and takes the picture once the card holds still; a saved photo or a screenshot works too. Scythe reads the words and the underlining and highlighting on the paper, then shows the result full screen, as the card it will be, to check and mark before saving. A card with no tag is saved as “Untitled”. Each draft waits on the team’s Scythe for the member who scanned it, on any device; only the words wait, never the picture.
  • Nudges. Optional reminders, off by default. Scythe checks once a day. A debater receives one after three days without cutting a card, then once a week while it stays quiet, four at most; a coach receives one when no debater on the team has cut a card in three days. They arrive as a notification on a laptop or phone; email reminders are coming soon. Enabling them shares only your username, team name, role and email. A small AI writes each one-line reminder from those details and the day count; it never sees a card.

Learning from the team

After a team has approved 50 cards, Scythe can learn from them and suggest underlining and highlighting on new cards, shown as dotted underlines. Press Enter to accept a suggestion or x to dismiss it for good. Scythe learns only from approved cards, changes nothing without your approval, and never alters a word.

Scythe also identifies the people, organizations, places and dates in each card, which speeds up writing cites.

Who a card names

  • Every name, identified. Scythe recognizes the people, places and organizations a card names: the world’s best-known people, countries, cities, governments, agencies, alliances, parties, think tanks, universities, newspapers, journals, treaties and wars, along with the authors and publications your team has cited. Point at a name to see who it is, such as “Barack Obama, 44th president of the United States”, and open its page on Wikidata.
  • Corrected by the team. For each name, a member can confirm it, choose someone else, or mark it as nobody identifiable or not a name. A correction applies to every card that names them, and each team keeps its own corrections in its own history.
  • Coming soon: checked before rounds. A newly cut card will not be approvable until every name in it has been checked, so approved evidence always says whom it quotes. This switches on once the iPhone app can check names too.

Who it is for

Policy, LD and PF debaters who cut their own evidence; coaches who manage a squad and decide which cards are ready for rounds; and partners preparing the same file the night before a tournament.
